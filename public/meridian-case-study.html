Product strategy case study Agentic systems · RegTech Working codename MERIDIAN Conceptual — not a shipped product

Reading the rules was never the hard part. Knowing which returns they break is.

An agent that watches the RBI's directions, maps each amendment to the returns, data fields and validation rules it touches, and hands the person who owns those returns a package they can sign. A chatbot that answers questions about the rules solves a different, easier problem.

The deliverable

What the reporting owner actually signs

01
the change

The regulator’s own wording, cited to source and date. The agent’s summary of it carries no weight in a review.

A package that paraphrases the regulator is a package nobody can defend in a review.

02
the mapping

Which returns, data fields and validation rules the change touches. Each one carries the basis for the claim.

Every mapping is a proposition, not an answer. The officer has to be able to reject one.

03
the delta

The current field definition or calculation against what the change now needs, diffed at the line.

Pitched at the level a reporting analyst edits. An executive summary is useless here.

04
the uncertainty

Where the interpretation is thin, stated plainly, with what would resolve it.

An agent that never admits doubt has told you nothing when it stays quiet.

05
the decision

Accept, amend, or refuse — attributed to a named human, before anything moves.

The one step the agent is never given. This is the whole product.

06
the record

Inputs, reasoning, the person, the timestamp — and the overrules.

A trail that only logs agreement proves nothing. The disagreements are the evidence.

The structure of the package. Field content is illustrative.

The bet

Most "AI for compliance" is a search box. This isn't.

The obvious build here is search over the RBI's directions with a chat window on top. "Ask me about the NBFC rules." It demos well and solves almost nothing. Nobody in a reporting team is stuck on understanding a rule. They're stuck on what comes after it. Which returns does it touch? Which fields, which validation rules, which source data? What has to change before the next filing? And can they show an auditor how they decided?

That work has the four properties that actually justify an agent rather than a workflow: multi-step planning across a living queue of changes, tool use across fragmented systems of record, state held across a review that spans days, and bounded autonomy with explicit human checkpoints. Drop any one and an agent is over-engineering. Meridian is scoped to the problem where all four hold.

Strategic wager

The product's defensibility is its restraint, not its autonomy.

The instinct is to maximize what the agent does end-to-end. In a domain where a wrong call is a personal-liability event for a named officer, the winning design does the opposite: it does the 80% of gathering, mapping, and drafting at a quality bar a human can verify in minutes — and stops, loudly, at every act of judgment. The moat is a trust boundary engineered so well that the person who signs the return will put their name on the output.

01 · The problem

The rulebook got tidier. It didn't get quieter.

In November 2025 the RBI withdrew 9,445 circulars and folded what was left into 244 Master Directions, one set per type of regulated entity. That was a real cleanup. It also reset the clock. Since then, changes have arrived as amendment directions on top of those Master Directions, and they haven't slowed down. The capital adequacy direction for commercial banks had been amended eleven times in 2026 by the end of October.

Reporting teams feel every change twice. First the rule moves. Then the return has to move with it. A field gets added or redefined, a validation rule changes, a calculation shifts. Today someone works that out by hand, holding an amendment up against a return template, a data dictionary and a pile of spreadsheets. That's structured, repetitive work, and it doesn't scale by hiring.

02 · Who feels it

One overloaded owner. One overloaded doer.

The economic buyer and the daily user are different people with different fears, and the product has to satisfy both. The buyer fears the miss; the user fears the busywork. Meridian's job is to take the busywork off the user in a way the buyer can trust under audit.

Buyer · economic

Financial Controller / Head of Regulatory Reporting

Owns the returns and answers for them. Can't hand off the sign-off, and knows it. Buys assurance and defensibility before efficiency. The skeptic to win.

User · daily

Regulatory reporting analyst

Reads the amendment, opens the return template, checks the data dictionary, then does it all again for the next one. Wants the first 80% done and trustworthy so they can spend judgment where it counts. The champion to delight.

The compliance officer still matters. They own what a rule means. Meridian hands them that question with the evidence attached. It doesn't answer it for them.

Job to be done: "When the RBI amends a direction, tell me fast and precisely which of my returns and fields it touches, draft the change, and give me a record I can defend — so nothing slips into the next filing while I'm buried in this quarter's close."

03 · Why now

Three curves crossed at once.

Capability. Models can now read regulatory prose and line it up against a structured list of returns, fields and validation rules. That translation step is what beat rules engines and kept this work manual. Agents let it run all the time instead of as a one-off query.

The rulebook. The consolidation gave every regulated entity a finite, current set of Master Directions, and changes now land as amendments to them. So "what exactly changed" is a clean diff for the first time. The RBI also publishes its list of returns, data definitions and validation rules. That means the thing Meridian maps to is public as well. I can build and test the mapping before any customer shares a single file.

The counter-intuitive one. Regulators are now looking at the AI itself. The RBI's FREE-AI framework (August 2025) sets out seven principles and 26 recommendations. It's advisory for now. But it expects banks and NBFCs to stay accountable for AI they buy from vendors, not just AI they build, starting with a board-approved AI policy. That favors a well-governed, fully logged agent and punishes a slick autonomous one.

Then there's DPDP. Its main obligations land on 13 May 2027. Meridian reads regulation text and report specs. It never needs borrower data, so it stays out of the personal-data path entirely.

Why now — what's changing right now

Three live changes, all landing on reporting.

NBFC returns, rewritten: on 31 July 2026 the RBI pulled NBFC supervisory returns into one new direction. Some returns were renamed, large-exposure reporting changed for the Upper Layer, and the board now owns data accuracy.

ECL for banks: issued 27 April 2026, live from 1 April 2027. It replaces incurred-loss provisioning for commercial banks, and it has already set off amendments across other directions, from capital adequacy to investment portfolios.

The amendment flow: eleven amendments to the bank capital adequacy direction alone in 2026. And the start dates aren't simple. Some amendments start on a fixed date. Others start when a bank opts in, or on a backstop date, whichever comes first.

No reporting team keeps every return in step with that by hand. That's the mapping problem.

04 · Strategy & wedge

Fill the gap between compliance and reporting.

Two kinds of tool already exist in India, and neither does this. Compliance platforms like OnFinance AI and Zango are funded, real and already have customers. They track changes, interpret them, assign owners and test controls. Their user is the compliance officer. Reporting vendors like IRIS and Profinch help firms produce and submit the returns. Their user is the reporting team, but they take the requirement as given.

What I can't find in either is the link between the two: this amendment, this return, this field, this validation rule. That's the gap. I've only seen their public material, so the first job is sitting through their demos and checking I'm right.

Beachhead: RBI supervisory returns at mid-sized NBFCs. Narrow on purpose. There are a lot of NBFCs, their reporting teams are small, and their returns were just rewritten. And because the RBI publishes the return list, data definitions and validation rules, I can prove the mapping on public data first. It's also the work I know best. I've spent years building regulatory reporting platforms. Land on change-to-return mapping. Expand to commercial banks as ECL goes live, then to SEBI and IRDAI.

Scope discipline — v1

What ships, and what deliberately doesn't.

In: monitoring RBI Master Directions and amendment directions · obligation extraction · mapping to returns, data fields, validation rules and the customer's own report logic · drafted impact notes, field-level redlines and remediation tickets · full audit log.

Out (on purpose): regulatory interpretation, risk acceptance, certifying or filing a return, and anything said to the RBI. These are governance acts. Shipping them would be the fastest way to lose the buyer's trust — and the regulator's.

05 · How it works

A six-stage loop with the human wired into the middle, not bolted on the end.

01

Watch

Monitor RBI notifications, Master Directions & amendment directions; detect new & changed paragraphs.

02

Interpret

Extract discrete obligations; classify by entity type, layer & effective date.

03

Map

Match each obligation to returns, fields, validation rules & the customer's report logic; score confidence.

04

Draft

Produce an impact note, field-level redlines & remediation tickets — every claim cited to source.

05

Sign-off

Analyst reviews; low-confidence maps forced into review. Human accepts, edits, or rejects.

06

Log

Immutable, reproducible record of inputs, reasoning, and the human decision.

06 · The hard part

The line between what the agent does and what a human owns is the product.

In this domain the dangerous failure isn't the obvious one — it's the quiet one: an output that looks right, passes a tired analyst's glance, and surfaces only when an auditor pulls the thread months later. The whole design exists to make that failure mode visible before sign-off, and to make every accepted decision reconstructable afterward.

So autonomy is capped by design. Anything below a confidence threshold can't be auto-cleared. Every drafted claim links to its source text. And the four acts of judgment below are never delegated — not because the model can't attempt them, but because accountability for them legally and ethically can't move to a model.

Meridian does — the 80%
  • Watch every source, continuously
  • Extract and classify obligations
  • Map changes to specific returns, fields & rules, with confidence scores
  • Draft summaries, redlines & tickets, each cited to source
  • Log everything for audit
The human owns — the judgment
  • What a rule means for this specific business
  • Whether a residual risk is accepted
  • Final sign-off, and certifying the return
  • Any material communication with a regulator

Boundary mirrors current practitioner guidance (e.g. SureCloud, Oliver Wyman, Deloitte, 2026): agents reliably handle monitoring, mapping, evidence and drafting; interpretation, risk acceptance and sign-off stay human. Mature deployments report agents carrying ~20–40% of repetitive compliance work — which is the realistic ceiling, not a slide-deck "fully autonomous."

07 · What breaks, and the design answer

Every one of these cost me something before I designed against it.

Hallucinated mappingSeverity: high · quiet

Agent confidently links a change to the wrong return or field, or invents one. Answer: confidence scoring with mandatory human review below threshold; mappings are suggestions until signed, never auto-applied; every claim cited to retrievable source text.

The missed changeSeverity: catastrophic

A relevant rule never enters the queue — the failure no one sees until it's a fine. Answer: source-coverage SLAs as a first-class, monitored metric; false-negative rate as the headline number; periodic human spot-audits of "no impact" verdicts.

Effective-date trapsSeverity: high · quiet

Not every amendment starts on one date. Some start on a fixed date. Some start when a bank opts in, with a backstop date if it doesn't. Tie a change to the wrong reporting cycle and the package is wrong while looking right. Answer: model start dates as rules, not a single field; show the start condition on every package; re-check open items when a customer's adoption date changes.

Stale report mapSeverity: medium

Mapping is only as good as the customer's map of returns to source data, and that drifts. Answer: treat that map as part of the product, since the customer won't keep it current; flag fields with no recent change; reconcile against drafted changes.

Automation complacencySeverity: high · cultural

Reviewers rubber-stamp because the agent is "usually right." Answer: design review for friction where it matters — surface disagreement and low confidence prominently; sample-inject known cases to keep reviewers honest.

We are governed AISeverity: high

The RBI's FREE-AI framework is advisory today, but it expects banks and NBFCs to answer for the AI they buy, not just the AI they build. Our customers will have to justify us to their own board. Answer: model cards, an internal eval harness on mapping precision/recall, explainability and full logging built as product, not compliance theater — and handed to whoever owns the customer's AI policy as a differentiator.

08 · How we'd know it works

What the eval has to catch before anyone trusts it.

Demo metrics ("changes processed!") are vanity. The metrics that matter are the ones the person signing the return would stake their name on — and the most important one is the rate of missed relevant changes, because that's the failure that ends careers. Business outcomes (fines avoided) are real but hard to attribute honestly; I'd track them as directional, not as a success gate.

Leading · quality

Mapping precision & recall

Against a human-labeled gold set. The number that decides whether the product is usable at all.

Leading · safety

False-negative rate

Relevant changes the agent missed. The catastrophic metric. Target asymptotically toward zero.

Leading · adoption

Sign-off throughput

Changes assessed per analyst per week — and the % cleared without edits as trust matures.

Lagging · value

Time-to-assess

From a change publishing to a human-signed verdict. Days → hours is the headline customer win.

Lagging · value

Analyst hours reclaimed

Hours moved from cross-referencing to judgment. The user's daily proof.

Lagging · business

Resubmissions & RBI data queries

Returns refiled or queried because a change was missed. Directional; attribution noted, not overclaimed.

North star

Verified regulatory changes assessed and correctly mapped, per analyst, per week — held at a target precision the customer trusts. It couples value (throughput) to safety (precision) so the team can't win on one by sacrificing the other.

09 · Go to market

Land on the miss-anxiety. Expand on the time saved.

Motion

  • Design partners first. 3–5 reporting teams at mid-sized NBFCs. Co-build the onboarding of their return-to-source map. That's the real adoption bottleneck.
  • Land on mapping RBI changes to NBFC supervisory returns. Sharpest pain, narrowest scope.
  • Expand to commercial banks ahead of ECL going live, then to SEBI and IRDAI, then into evidence and attestation once sign-off trust is earned.
  • Earn the buyer with the audit trail and model cards. Sell defensibility, not autonomy.

Positioning & pricing

  • vs. OnFinance AI / Zango: they tell the compliance officer what changed and who owns it. We tell the reporting owner what breaks in their returns.
  • vs. reporting vendors (IRIS, Profinch): they help produce and file the return. We tell you which return to change, and why, before the next cycle.
  • Pricing: per entity, tiered by the number of returns covered. Priced for a mid-sized NBFC's budget, not a big bank's.
  • Content moat answer: there isn't one, and that's fine. The RBI's directions and return specs are public and now consolidated. The mapping is the product.

10 · Sequencing

A phased path that de-risks trust before it scales reach.

Phase 0~Quarter 1

Prove the mapping bar on public data

Before building UI: take the RBI's published returns, data definitions and validation rules, plus a year of amendment directions. Label which returns and fields each amendment actually touched. Test whether mapping precision/recall clears a usable bar. If it can't, nothing else matters. Riskiest assumption, checked first.

Phase 1~Quarter 2–4

NBFC beachhead, with design partners

Two or three NBFC reporting teams run it on their own return-to-source maps. The full six-stage loop, with sign-off and audit log. Win daily use and the first reference customer.

Phase 2~Year 2

Commercial banks

Take the same engine to banks as ECL goes live and the amendment flow keeps coming. Same loop, a bigger set of returns.

Phase 3~Year 2–3

More regulators, then evidence

Add SEBI and IRDAI, then extend the proven trust boundary into evidence collection and attestation. Outside India after that, where the mapping engine, not local content, is the advantage.

11 · Reflection

If I could only validate one thing before writing a line of product code, it's whether mapping precision can hit a bar the person signing the return trusts.

Everything downstream — the UI, the GTM, the roadmap — is worthless if step 03 can't reliably tell a customer which returns and fields a change touches. That's why Phase 0 is an offline eval on public RBI data. A prototype would prove nothing yet. The cheapest way to kill or confirm the whole thesis.

The strategic bet I'd defend in a room: in a liability-bearing domain, restraint is the product. The temptation will always be to push the autonomy further to look more impressive. The discipline to stop the agent precisely at the four acts of judgment — and to make the stopping legible and auditable — is what earns the signature on the return, and a signature is the only thing that makes any of this real.

What this case study is meant to show: problem framing from real domain pain · a defensible scope decision under ambiguity · technical fluency without pretending to be the engineer · honest metrics including the ones that are hard to attribute · and a point of view on where AI should not act.
