Agents at a control boundary

The operating problem

Compliance review queues were running behind. That's the plain version. Work arrived faster than it cleared, and the backlog compounded, because every day of lag made the next day's triage harder.

I sat with reviewers and watched what the job actually consisted of. Open a case. Pull the underlying records from whichever system holds them. Cross-reference against the obligation that applies in that jurisdiction. Check the record is complete. Decide. Write down why. Most of the clock went to retrieval and assembly. The judgment call took the smallest slice of it, and the judgment call was the part that needed a person.

The obvious answer is more reviewers. I pushed back on that. Hiring scales cost with volume and volume kept climbing. New reviewers take a long time to become reliable in a regulated domain, and until they are, someone senior is checking their work, so early capacity is negative. And every additional pair of hands is another source of variance in how the same obligation gets interpreted. In an audit, variance is the thing that hurts you.

So the target was the retrieval and assembly layer. Get the case fully built, evidence attached, obligation matched, draft rationale written, before a human ever looks at it. Keep the human on the decision. That framing survived contact with reality, which is more than I can say for the timeline.

Architecture

I built the system out of reusable primitives rather than as a single application, because I knew more workflows were coming and I didn't want to rebuild the plumbing each time.

The rule underneath all of it: the model proposes, the orchestrator commits. An agent can read, reason, draft, and recommend. It cannot write to an enterprise system of record on its own authority. Every state change goes through the orchestration layer, which validates the payload against a schema, checks preconditions, and records what happened and why. That's what makes the execution deterministic in the sense that matters here. The language part is probabilistic. The action part isn't. Given the same validated output, the same thing happens every time, and it happens in a way an auditor can replay.

None of this would have been affordable on a fragmented data layer. It sits on top of a unified Regulatory Reporting Platform I'd led earlier, in 2022 and 2023, which pulled reporting onto common rails: 60+ regulatory reports onboarded across three markets in three months, a 60%+ operational efficiency gain, and an 80% reduction in reporting turnaround time. That work gave me a single place where the data model, the lineage, and the controls already lived. Agents need a clean substrate more than they need a clever prompt. Pointing this layer at the mess that existed before would have produced a very impressive demo and nothing an auditor would accept.

Because the pieces were primitives rather than a bespoke build, other teams could pick them up. That's how the decision pipelines spread beyond the workflow I started with, and how the operating model shifted from manual execution to system-driven. It's also why the reliability patterns had to be in the primitives themselves. Anything optional gets skipped by whoever adopts it next.

  • Scoped LLM agentsNarrow agents with a defined job, a defined input contract, and permission to say they don't know. Broad general-purpose agents were harder to evaluate and harder to defend in review, so I kept the scope tight and composed them instead.
  • MCP-based connectorsTyped, declarative access to enterprise systems. Each connector exposes a small surface with explicit inputs and outputs. Adding a system means writing a connector, not rewriting an agent, and permissions live at the connector boundary where security can reason about them.
  • Orchestration layerOwns control flow, state, ordering, and the write path. Handles retries with backoff and idempotency keys. Enforces preconditions before any action lands. This is where the determinism lives.
  • Structured outputsEvery agent returns schema-bound output, never free text that something downstream has to parse hopefully. If it doesn't validate, it doesn't proceed. I want schema violations loud. Papering over them buries the failure that matters.
  • Validation layersChecks that run between the model and the action: schema conformance, grounding of each assertion to a source record, sanity checks on the obligation match. Cheap to run, and they catch a category of error the model can't catch about itself.
  • Human-in-the-loop overrideA reviewer can reject, edit, or escalate at the gate, and their action is captured as labeled data. Overrides are the main feedback channel back into the evals.

How the evals were designed

I built the labeled set out of historical closed cases, where the correct answer was already known and already defensible, and held part of it back so I wasn't grading on cases anyone had tuned against. Sampling was stratified by jurisdiction and by case type, because the aggregate number hides the jurisdiction where the thing quietly falls apart.

I measured per step, not only end to end. Did the connector fetch the right records. Did the agent match the right obligation. Did the output validate against schema. Was every assertion in the rationale traceable to a source record. When the end-to-end result was wrong, I wanted to know which link broke, because a retrieval failure and a reasoning failure need completely different fixes and look identical from the outside.

The metric I cared most about was recall on missed obligations. The costs here are wildly asymmetric. A false positive means a reviewer reads a case that turns out fine. Annoying, and cheap. A miss means an obligation that should have been caught wasn't, and nobody finds out until someone external asks. Precision I could buy back later with better filtering and better ranking. Recall I couldn't buy back at all, because the system never surfaced the case in the first place, so no amount of downstream review would have found it. So I tuned toward over-flagging and treated reviewer load as the budget I was spending.

Abstention counted as a good outcome. An agent that returns low confidence and routes to a human is behaving correctly. I tracked how often that happened and on which case types, and a rising abstention rate on a specific type was usually the earliest signal that something upstream had changed.

Prompts and configs were versioned like code, and every eval run was pinned to a version. The whole suite re-ran on any prompt change, any model change, any connector change. I also kept a regression set of every failure the system had ever made, which grew and never shrank. Nothing shipped that regressed it.

Shadow mode

It ran in shadow mode before any decision was acted on. Cases went to the humans as normal. The system worked the same cases in parallel and wrote its answer somewhere the reviewers couldn't see it. Nothing it produced touched a queue, a record, or a filing.

Raw agreement rate was the least interesting thing I got out of it. High agreement mostly tells you the easy cases are easy. What I wanted was the shape of the disagreements.

So every disagreement got pulled and read by a person, and sorted into buckets. Agent wrong. Human wrong. Both defensible under the same obligation text. Question badly posed. That last bucket was the one I didn't expect to be as full as it was. Some obligations were genuinely ambiguous, and different reviewers had been resolving them different ways for a long time without anyone noticing, because nothing had ever compared their outputs side by side. Building the system surfaced an interpretation problem that predated the system. Some of the most useful output from that phase was written guidance for humans rather than changes to the agent.

I also checked stability. Same case, run again, does the answer hold. An agent that flips on identical input is unusable at a control boundary, whatever its average accuracy looks like, and you only see that if you deliberately test for it.

Shadow mode also gave me the operational picture: latency under real load, connector error rates against systems that have maintenance windows and rate limits, cost per case. And it let me practise the audit story before it mattered. I could point at a case and walk someone through what the system saw, what it concluded, and why, using the same trail I'd have to produce under scrutiny.

Failure modes

Agents fail in specific ways at a control boundary. Most of them are boring, and none of them announce themselves. These are the ones I actually designed against.

  • Silent plausible-wrong outputThe worst one, and the reason grounding checks exist. The agent produces a well-formed, confident, internally consistent answer that happens to be wrong. It reads better than a correct answer written by a tired human. There's no error, no exception, no flag. The mitigation is forcing every assertion to cite the source record it came from, and validating that the citation actually says what the assertion claims.
  • Reviewer rubber-stampingThe one that keeps me up. A human gate people stop reading is worse than no gate at all, because it manufactures a signed audit trail attesting to review that didn't happen. And it degrades quietly: the system gets good, approvals become routine, click-through gets faster, and the gate is decorative long before anyone says so. I attacked it from several directions. Reviewers record a reason on approve, not only on reject. Approve latency gets tracked, and suspiciously fast approvals get sampled. Known-bad cases get injected into the queue and the catch rate on those is a real metric. The interface leads with evidence and uncertainty rather than with the conclusion. None of this solves it permanently. It's a thing you monitor forever.
  • Schema driftAn upstream system adds a field, changes an enum, renames something. The connector still returns data, the agent still returns something that validates, and the meaning has shifted underneath. Strict schemas at the connector boundary catch the structural half. Contract tests against real systems catch more. The subtle cases still need someone paying attention.
  • Retry stormsA dependency slows down, retries stack, load increases, the dependency slows further. Naive retry logic turns a small blip into an outage and can double-file work if the write path isn't idempotent. Backoff, jitter, per-dependency circuit breakers, capped attempts, idempotency keys on every write. Then a hard rule: when retries are exhausted, the case parks in a queue for a human. It never guesses its way forward.
  • Context stalenessThe agent reasons over a snapshot. Records change. Obligations change. A cached lookup or a long-running case means the conclusion was correct against a world that no longer exists. I timestamped inputs, expired context aggressively, and re-validated key facts immediately before any action landed rather than trusting what was fetched at the start.
  • Scope creep through capabilityOnce it works, people want it pointed at adjacent problems that look similar and aren't. The evals were built for a specific case distribution. Run it somewhere else and every number you have is decorative. New domain, new labeled set, new shadow run, no exceptions.

What I'd do differently

Honest list, in rough order of how much it cost me.

I built agents before I built the eval harness. For a stretch I was judging output by reading it, which feels productive and is close to worthless. I couldn't tell improvement from noise, and I made changes I now suspect did nothing. I'd write the labeled set and the harness before the demo, even though a labeled set is unglamorous work that impresses nobody in a demo.

I under-invested in the reviewer interface. I treated it as a thin layer over the agent's output, and it led with the conclusion. That's exactly the layout that trains rubber-stamping. Evidence should come first, uncertainty should be visible, and the conclusion should arrive after the reviewer has already formed a view. I fixed it later. It should have been in the design from the start, because reviewer behaviour is part of the system and I should have designed for it.

I assumed obligation definitions were solid ground truth. They weren't, and shadow mode is where I found out. I'd run a small human-versus-human agreement check before building anything, on the same cases, to measure how much the humans agree with each other. That number sets a realistic ceiling and would have reframed the early conversations about accuracy targets.

I went wide on connectors too early. Breadth across systems demoed well and meant several integrations were shallow at the same time, so debugging always crossed a boundary I only half understood. Depth on fewer systems, then expand.

I didn't define exit criteria for shadow mode in advance. Because there was no pre-agreed bar, the decision to move forward became a judgement call under pressure, and every stakeholder brought their own private threshold to it. Writing that bar down beforehand costs almost nothing and removes an argument you will otherwise have at the worst possible moment.

And I framed the early pitch around automation. That made people hear job replacement, and it put me on the back foot in rooms I needed on my side. The work moved reviewers off retrieval and onto judgment, which is the more accurate description and also the one that would have got me more goodwill sooner. I got the framing right eventually. I'd rather have got it right at the start.
