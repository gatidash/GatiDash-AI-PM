Product strategy case study Agentic systems · RegTech Working codename MERIDIAN Conceptual — not a shipped product

Reading the rules was never the hard part. Proving which control each one touches is.

An agent that watches every regulator you answer to, maps each change to the specific internal controls it breaks, and hands a compliance officer a defensible package to sign — not a chatbot that answers questions about the law.

// the core job, in one diagram

The bet

Most "AI for compliance" is a search box. This isn't.

The reflexive build for this space is retrieval over a regulatory corpus with a chat window on top — "ask questions about the AML/CTF rules." It demos well and solves almost nothing, because the bottleneck inside a compliance team was never understanding a rule. It's the orchestration after: finding every internal control, policy, and data flow the rule touches; deciding what has to change; and producing an audit trail that survives a regulator's scrutiny.

That work has the four properties that actually justify an agent rather than a workflow: multi-step planning across a living queue of changes, tool use across fragmented systems of record, state held across a review that spans days, and bounded autonomy with explicit human checkpoints. Drop any one and an agent is over-engineering. Meridian is scoped to the problem where all four hold.

Strategic wager

The product's defensibility is its restraint, not its autonomy.

The instinct is to maximize what the agent does end-to-end. In a domain where a wrong call is a personal-liability event for a named officer, the winning design does the opposite: it does the 80% of gathering, mapping, and drafting at a quality bar a human can verify in minutes — and stops, loudly, at every act of judgment. The moat is a trust boundary engineered so well that a Chief Compliance Officer will put their name on the output.

01 · The problem

A volume problem that humans structurally cannot win.

Global regulators now publish on the order of 200+ changes a day — roughly 56,000 a year across more than a thousand bodies. A regulated firm doesn't care about most of them, but it cannot know which ones matter without reading all of them. Industry surveys put a quarter of financial firms at a full working day every week spent just tracking change, before a single control is updated.

The cost of getting it wrong has detached from the cost of doing it. Global compliance fines ran to billions in 2025, and the average cost of a single non-compliance event in 2026 is estimated in the eight figures. Meanwhile the actual labor — cross-referencing a circular against a control library held in spreadsheets, ticketing systems, and someone's head — is exactly the structured, repetitive orchestration that doesn't scale by hiring.

02 · Who feels it

One overloaded owner. One overloaded doer.

The economic buyer and the daily user are different people with different fears, and the product has to satisfy both. The buyer fears the miss; the user fears the busywork. Meridian's job is to take the busywork off the user in a way the buyer can trust under audit.

Buyer · economic

Head of Compliance / GRC lead

Accountable to the board and the regulator. Cannot delegate the sign-off, and knows it. Buys assurance and defensibility before efficiency. The skeptic to win.

User · daily

Compliance analyst

Drowning in alerts, doing the cross-referencing by hand. Wants the first 80% done and trustworthy so they can spend judgment where it counts. The champion to delight.

Job to be done: "When a regulator publishes something, tell me fast and precisely which of my controls it touches, draft the change, and give me a record I can defend — so nothing slips while I'm buried in last quarter's audit."

03 · Why now

Three curves crossed at once.

Capability. Models can now read unstructured regulatory prose and align it to a structured control taxonomy — the exact translation step that defeated rules engines and kept this manual. Agentic orchestration lets that run continuously across systems instead of as a one-off query.

Economics. The volume-and-fines curve has made manual tracking indefensible, and regulatory intelligence is among the fastest-growing slices of a RegTech market on track from the low-$20Bs in 2026 toward $100B+ within a decade — with financial-crime and AML supervision, the remit of regulators like AUSTRAC, MAS, and the EU’s CSSF, among the densest and fastest-moving.

The counter-intuitive one. Regulators are now scrutinizing the AI itself — the EU AI Act treats compliance AI as high-risk, and buyers increasingly demand model cards and verifiable audit trails over black-box output. That favors a well-governed, fully-logged agent and punishes a slick autonomous one. The regulatory tide that threatens careless AI is a tailwind for a product built around traceability from day one.

04 · Strategy & wedge

Win a narrow beachhead the incumbents ignore.

The incumbents — Thomson Reuters, MetricStream — own regulatory content and sell it into manual workflow. The newer compliance-automation players (Vanta, Drata) automate static frameworks like SOC 2, not the live firehose of jurisdictional change. Neither does continuous change-to-control mapping as an agentic loop, and both are US/EU-first.

Beachhead: financial-crime & AML change across three tier-1 regulators — AUSTRAC (AU), MAS (SG), and the CSSF (LU/EU). Narrow by problem, not just geography: the AML and cross-border obligations every global fintech and bank answers to, where a missed change is a personal-liability event and even Western-built tools still bolt the mapping on by hand. It's also where I have direct operating context in global payments. Land on change-to-control mapping; expand into evidence collection and policy attestation once the trust boundary is proven.

Scope discipline — v1

What ships, and what deliberately doesn't.

In: continuous monitoring of a defined source set · obligation extraction · mapping to the customer's existing control library · drafted impact summaries, control redlines, and remediation tickets · full audit log.

Out (on purpose): regulatory interpretation, risk acceptance, final sign-off, and material regulator communications. These are governance acts. Shipping them would be the fastest way to lose the buyer's trust — and the regulator's.

05 · How it works

A six-stage loop with the human wired into the middle, not bolted on the end.

01

Watch

Monitor a defined set of regulators, gazettes & feeds; detect new & amended items.

02

Interpret

Extract discrete obligations; classify by domain, jurisdiction & effective date.

03

Map

Match each obligation to the customer's control library; score confidence.

04

Draft

Produce an impact summary, control redlines & remediation tickets — every claim cited to source.

05

Sign-off

Analyst reviews; low-confidence maps forced into review. Human accepts, edits, or rejects.

06

Log

Immutable, reproducible record of inputs, reasoning, and the human decision.

06 · The hard part

The line between what the agent does and what a human owns is the product.

In this domain the dangerous failure isn't the obvious one — it's the quiet one: an output that looks right, passes a tired analyst's glance, and surfaces only when an auditor pulls the thread months later. The whole design exists to make that failure mode visible before sign-off, and to make every accepted decision reconstructable afterward.

So autonomy is capped by design. Anything below a confidence threshold can't be auto-cleared. Every drafted claim links to its source text. And the four acts of judgment below are never delegated — not because the model can't attempt them, but because accountability for them legally and ethically can't move to a model.

Meridian does — the 80%
  • Watch every source, continuously
  • Extract and classify obligations
  • Map changes to specific controls, with confidence scores
  • Draft summaries, redlines & tickets, each cited to source
  • Log everything for audit
The human owns — the judgment
  • What a rule means for this specific business
  • Whether a residual risk is accepted
  • Final compliance sign-off
  • Any material communication with a regulator

Boundary mirrors current practitioner guidance (e.g. SureCloud, Oliver Wyman, Deloitte, 2026): agents reliably handle monitoring, mapping, evidence and drafting; interpretation, risk acceptance and sign-off stay human. Mature deployments report agents carrying ~20–40% of repetitive compliance work — which is the realistic ceiling, not a slide-deck "fully autonomous."

07 · What breaks, and the design answer

Naming the failure modes is the senior move.

Hallucinated mappingSeverity: high · quiet

Agent confidently links a change to the wrong control, or invents one. Answer: confidence scoring with mandatory human review below threshold; mappings are suggestions until signed, never auto-applied; every claim cited to retrievable source text.

The missed changeSeverity: catastrophic

A relevant rule never enters the queue — the failure no one sees until it's a fine. Answer: source-coverage SLAs as a first-class, monitored metric; false-negative rate is the headline number, not a footnote; periodic human spot-audits of "no impact" verdicts.

Stale control librarySeverity: medium

Mapping is only as good as the customer's control inventory, which drifts. Answer: treat library hygiene as part of the product, not the customer's problem; flag controls with no recent activity; reconcile against drafted changes.

Automation complacencySeverity: high · cultural

Reviewers rubber-stamp because the agent is "usually right." Answer: design review for friction where it matters — surface disagreement and low confidence prominently; sample-inject known cases to keep reviewers honest.

We are regulated AISeverity: existential

Our own system is high-risk AI under emerging law. Answer: model cards, an internal eval harness on mapping precision/recall, explainability and full logging built as product, not compliance theater — and sold as a differentiator.

08 · How we'd know it works

Measure the miss, not the magic.

Demo metrics ("changes processed!") are vanity. The metrics that matter are the ones a CCO would stake their name on — and the most important one is the rate of missed relevant changes, because that's the failure that ends careers. Business outcomes (fines avoided) are real but hard to attribute honestly; I'd track them as directional, not as a success gate.

Leading · quality

Mapping precision & recall

Against a human-labeled gold set. The number that decides whether the product is usable at all.

Leading · safety

False-negative rate

Relevant changes the agent missed. The catastrophic metric. Target asymptotically toward zero.

Leading · adoption

Sign-off throughput

Changes assessed per analyst per week — and the % cleared without edits as trust matures.

Lagging · value

Time-to-assess

From a change publishing to a human-signed verdict. Days → hours is the headline customer win.

Lagging · value

Analyst hours reclaimed

Hours moved from cross-referencing to judgment. The user's daily proof.

Lagging · business

Audit-prep time

Effort to produce a defensible trail at audit. Directional; attribution noted, not overclaimed.

North star

Verified regulatory changes assessed and correctly mapped, per analyst, per week — held at a target precision the customer trusts. It couples value (throughput) to safety (precision) so the team can't win on one by sacrificing the other.

09 · Go to market

Land on the miss-anxiety. Expand on the time saved.

Motion

  • Design partners first. 3–5 compliance teams at global fintechs and banks that answer to AUSTRAC, MAS, or the CSSF; co-build the control-library onboarding, which is the real adoption bottleneck.
  • Land on continuous change-to-control mapping for AUSTRAC, MAS, and CSSF AML obligations — the sharpest pain, narrowest scope.
  • Expand into evidence collection and attestation tracking once sign-off trust is established.
  • Earn the buyer with the audit trail and model cards — sell defensibility, not autonomy.

Positioning & pricing

  • vs. Thomson Reuters / MetricStream: they sell content into manual workflow; we close the loop to your controls, automatically.
  • vs. Vanta / Drata: they automate static frameworks; we track live jurisdictional change.
  • Pricing: seat + source-coverage tiers; priced to undercut enterprise incumbents and reach mid-market teams the incumbents can't serve economically.
  • Content moat answer: partner for raw regulatory feeds early rather than rebuild a 750-regulator corpus; compete on the mapping intelligence, not the firehose.

10 · Sequencing

A phased path that de-risks trust before it scales reach.

Phase 0~Quarter 1–2

Prove the mapping bar

Before building UI: assemble a human-labeled eval set of changes→controls and test whether mapping precision/recall can clear a usable threshold. If it can't, nothing else matters. Riskiest assumption, validated first.

Phase 1~Quarter 2–4

Change-to-control mapping, GA in beachhead

The six-stage loop for AUSTRAC, MAS, and CSSF, with sign-off and audit log. Win the design partners' daily use and the first reference CCO.

Phase 2~Year 2

Evidence & attestation

Extend the proven trust boundary into evidence collection and policy-attestation cycles — the natural adjacency once sign-off is trusted.

Phase 3~Year 2–3

Multi-jurisdiction

Expand the source set into the US, the UK, and the rest of APAC and the EU, where the agentic mapping engine — not regional content — is the durable advantage.

11 · Reflection

If I could only validate one thing before writing a line of product code, it's whether mapping precision can hit a bar a CCO trusts.

Everything downstream — the UI, the GTM, the roadmap — is worthless if step 03 can't reliably tell a customer which controls a change touches. That's why Phase 0 is an offline eval against labeled data, not a prototype. The cheapest way to kill or confirm the whole thesis.

The strategic bet I'd defend in a room: in a liability-bearing domain, restraint is the product. The temptation will always be to push the autonomy further to look more impressive. The discipline to stop the agent precisely at the four acts of judgment — and to make the stopping legible and auditable — is what earns a compliance officer's signature, and a signature is the only thing that makes any of this real.

What this case study is meant to show: problem framing from real domain pain · a defensible scope decision under ambiguity · technical fluency without pretending to be the engineer · honest metrics including the ones that are hard to attribute · and a point of view on where AI should not act.
